The Angel Does Not Take the Cup

On help, desire and the will in Gethsemane

A reading of Maximus the Confessor’s “Opusculum 6: On the Two Wills of Christ in the Agony of Gethsemane” (c. 640–41) and Joseph Ratzinger’s “Kneeling (prostratio)” in The Spirit of the Liturgy (2000).

Christ is brought down upon the ground while the cup remains before him and Peter, James and John sleep nearby. An angel appears to strengthen him without taking the cup away, and the garden therefore presents two material facts on which everything else depends, a body that cannot remain upright and a liquid held within a vessel, through which the will becomes visible.

The angel and the bloody sweat belong to two verses whose earliest attestation is disputed, since they are absent from several important witnesses and are double-bracketed in the Nestle-Aland text. One plausible account of their omission is apologetic, because some copyists could not bear a Christ who required strengthening, and Epiphanius already knew the passage as a place where copies had been corrected. The manuscript tradition thus carries within itself the scandal of the scene, in which the humanity of the Son needs help.1

Ratzinger begins from posture, noting that in Matthew and Mark Christ falls upon the ground while Luke has him kneel. He reads the gesture as Christ allowing himself to fall into human fallenness and praying “out of the lowest depths of human dereliction and anguish”. The body therefore does not illustrate an inward decision already complete but discloses a situation in which weight can no longer be carried as before, life recoils from its threatened destruction, and dependence becomes visible.2

The earth neither answers the prayer nor removes its object, but bears the body, so support first appears as that upon which weight may be yielded without the person being taken over. The will is not a hidden faculty issuing commands but the disposition of the whole living person as it appears in posture, pressure, fatigue, desire and address.

“Father, if you are willing, remove this cup from me.” The petition does not become faithful by being silenced, because Christ gives the recoil from death a definite address and allows desire to enter relation without being concealed or made sovereign.

Hebrews says that in the days of his flesh Christ offered prayers with loud cries and tears and “was heard”, and yet the cup remained. The verse is difficult because being heard may name deliverance through death rather than exemption from dying, while Hebrews need not be commenting directly on Luke. Read beside the garden, however, it prevents the simple conclusion that the prayer failed, since the petition is answered in the register of capacity rather than circumstance and what must be faced is not removed, while what is required to face it is given.3

Ratzinger says that Christ “takes up all the hesitation of the human will”, although taken strictly, hesitation comes close to what Maximus excludes. Maximus instead distinguishes the natural will, thelēma physikon, from gnomic or deliberative willing, gnōmē and prohairesis. The natural will is the nature’s appetite for being, life and its proper good, while gnomic willing weighs possibilities under conditions of incomplete knowledge, vacillation and uncertainty, which is why Maximus in his mature Christology denies gnomic will in Christ.45

Christ’s recoil is therefore not indecision, since he does not deliberate between obedience and disobedience or wonder whether the Father is good. Human nature turns from the death through which filial love must now pass, and removing this recoil would dehumanise rather than sanctify it. Maximus therefore insists both that the declining of the cup is genuinely willed and that “not what I will” excludes opposition, manifesting the concurrence of Christ’s human will with the divine will without abolishing their distinction.6

Gestalt language can offer a phenomenological correspondence rather than a second Christology, since Perls, Hefferline and Goodman locate experience at the boundary between organism and environment, and what Maximus names the natural will appears phenomenologically as the movement of the whole living person towards life in contact with what threatens it.7

A cup is not sheer openness because it receives by possessing a rim, and so it gives suffering a boundary in a portion that can be approached and named. Jesus himself joins cup and water when he asks James and John whether they can drink his cup and undergo his baptism, so the Passion appears both as a portion received and as an immersion through which one must pass.

The cup gathers the liquid realities of the garden and Passion in sweat, tears, blood and the wine already given at supper. The one who receives the cup becomes, in a precise sense, the vessel in which what is given can be held, yet reception is neither vacancy nor absorption because a bounded person receives a bounded portion and must still make a movement with it.

Peter, James and John had seen Christ transfigured, but now they are asked to remain and keep watch. Their physical nearness does not make them available, and Luke says that they sleep from grief, so their failure remains real without being reducible to indifference.

The garden therefore contains more than one failure of help because presence may fail either by occupying another’s act or by becoming absent within proximity. The disciples’ grief makes them unavailable, whereas the angel’s presence makes Christ more available to his own prayer, with the angel remaining present without becoming central while the disciples remain near without being able to remain with him.

Luke’s sequence is exact in that the angel strengthens Christ while Christ remains in agony, prays more earnestly, and sweats like drops of blood falling to the ground. The answer does not lessen the agony but enlarges Christ’s capacity to remain within it, so strengthening funds a more intense prayer rather than an escape from bodily extremity, and the will becomes capable of its human act precisely by being strengthened.

Whatever judgement is made about the passage’s earliest textual form, the angel and the bloody sweat share one textual fate and have been received together. The help Christ receives does not make him less exposed but adds capacity at the point where capacity has failed.

Mark the Monk writes that baptismal grace “becomes active” as the commandments are kept and “never ceases secretly to help us”, so human action does not complete a deficient grace, nor does grace make action superfluous. In the garden, the angel neither supplies a better decision nor performs the prayer, but strengthens the human being whose act must remain his own.8

This creaturely assistance also opens towards the Johannine promise of “another Advocate”. The angel is not the Holy Spirit, yet his manner of help prefigures the help of the Paraclete as one who abides alongside, teaches, recalls and enables without becoming a substitute for the human witness. Their identities remain distinct, while the resemblance lies in the form of assistance.9

Dionysius called Christ’s activity a “new theandric energy”, and Maximus receives the phrase while refusing any confusion of the divine and human operations, since one Christ acts through both natures while each remains what it is. Gethsemane gives this grammar bodily form because divine strength and human action cannot be divided into percentages, and although the strength is given, the prayer, the sweat and the rising remain human.10

The cup comes to Christ under two irreducible descriptions, since as violence it is filled by Judas, the authorities and the executioners, while as the concrete vocation to remain Son within what they do, it is received from the Father. The Father does not give betrayal, torture or murder but gives the Son the vocation to remain in filial love within the violence that human freedom is permitted to inflict.

Maximus distinguishes what God wills by good pleasure from what enters providence by economy or concession, a distinction that does not call evil secretly good but protects the difference between God’s saving purpose and the acts that purpose takes up without authoring. The cup is therefore not the catalogue of injuries but the form of filial fidelity within them, and while violence attempts to extract Christ’s life, the Son gives what his enemies can only try to take.11

The distinction must remain absolute wherever Gethsemane is brought near human suffering because no one may appoint another person’s suffering as their cup. An assailant does not become the Father by forcing suffering upon someone, and where harm is imposed, restraint is not accompaniment but complicity, so removal is the duty. Nothing here underwrites an appeal to another person’s freedom as a reason not to intervene against abuse. Help must not appropriate the act that belongs to the person being helped, while protection must interrupt the act that belongs to the person harming them.

Luke says that Christ rises from prayer, using the verb anastas, which belongs both to ordinary rising and to Luke’s vocabulary of resurrection. The resonance should not be turned into a prediction, yet it is difficult not to hear Easter faintly within a movement whereby strength passes into balance, muscle, orientation and step, so that although the cup remains bitter, the body stands and grace becomes visible as gait.12

The cup of his suffering becomes the cup of our salvation neither because suffering saves by its quantity nor because endurance makes violence holy. What is inflicted upon him enters an act that the violence itself cannot produce, because Christ receives from the Father the form of fidelity, bears human malice without allowing it to determine the meaning of his act, and gives his life where others intend only to take it.

The angel does not take the cup, and Christ rises to receive it himself. The ground has borne his body and the cup has given his suffering a form, while strength has been added without altering the demand or taking the movement from him.
