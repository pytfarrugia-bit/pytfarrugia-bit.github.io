The Angel Does Not Take the Cup

On help, desire and the will in Gethsemane

A reading of Maximus the Confessor’s “Opusculum 6: On the Two Wills of Christ in the Agony of Gethsemane” and Joseph Ratzinger’s “Kneeling (prostratio)” in The Spirit of the Liturgy.

Christ is brought down upon the ground. The cup remains before him. Peter, James and John sleep nearby. An angel appears and strengthens him, but does not take the cup away. The garden gives two material facts on which everything else depends: a body that cannot remain upright, and a liquid held within a vessel. The will becomes visible through both.

The angel and the bloody sweat belong to two verses whose earliest attestation is disputed. They are absent from several important witnesses and are double-bracketed in the Nestle-Aland text. One plausible account of their omission is apologetic: some copyists could not bear a Christ who required strengthening. Epiphanius already knew the passage as a place where copies had been corrected. The manuscript tradition thus carries the scandal of the scene itself: the humanity of the Son needs help.1

The earth receives him

Ratzinger begins from posture. In Matthew and Mark, Christ falls upon the ground; Luke has him kneel. Ratzinger reads the gesture as Christ allowing himself to fall into human fallenness and praying “out of the lowest depths of human dereliction and anguish”. The body does not illustrate an inward decision already complete. It discloses the situation. Weight can no longer be carried as before; life recoils from its threatened destruction; dependence becomes visible.2

The earth does not answer the prayer or remove its object. It bears the body. Support appears first as that upon which weight may be yielded without the person being taken over. The will here is not a hidden faculty issuing commands. It is the disposition of the whole living person, appearing in posture, pressure, fatigue, desire and address.

He asks that it be taken

“Father, if you are willing, remove this cup from me.” The petition does not become faithful by being silenced. Christ gives the recoil from death a definite address; desire enters relation without being concealed or made sovereign.

Hebrews says that, in the days of his flesh, Christ offered prayers with loud cries and tears and “was heard”. Yet the cup remained. The verse is difficult: being heard may name deliverance through death rather than exemption from dying, and Hebrews need not be commenting directly on Luke. Read beside the garden, however, it prevents the simple conclusion that the prayer failed. The petition is answered in the register of capacity rather than circumstance. What must be faced is not removed; what is required to face it is given.3

The natural will

Ratzinger says that Christ “takes up all the hesitation of the human will”. Taken strictly, hesitation comes close to what Maximus excludes. Maximus distinguishes the natural will, thelēma physikon, from gnomic or deliberative willing, gnōmē and prohairesis. The natural will is the nature’s appetite for being, life and its proper good. Gnomic willing weighs possibilities under the conditions of incomplete knowledge, vacillation and uncertainty. In his mature Christology Maximus denies gnomic will in Christ.45

There is recoil without indecision. Christ does not deliberate between obedience and disobedience, nor wonder whether the Father is good. Human nature turns from the death through which filial love must now pass. This recoil need not be sinful; removing it would dehumanise rather than sanctify. Maximus therefore insists both that the declining of the cup is genuinely willed and that “not what I will” excludes opposition, manifesting the concurrence of Christ’s human will with the divine will without abolishing their distinction.6

Gestalt language offers a phenomenological correspondence, not a second Christology. Perls, Hefferline and Goodman locate experience at the boundary between organism and environment. What Maximus names the natural will appears phenomenologically as the movement of the whole living person towards life in contact with what threatens it.7

The cup holds a portion

A cup is not sheer openness; it receives by possessing a rim. It gives suffering a boundary: this portion, approached and named. Jesus himself joins cup and water when he asks James and John whether they can drink his cup and undergo his baptism. The Passion is both a portion received and an immersion through which one must pass.

The cup gathers the liquid realities of the garden and Passion: sweat, tears, blood and the wine already given at supper. The one who receives the cup becomes, in a precise sense, the vessel in which what is given can be held. Reception is neither vacancy nor absorption. A bounded person receives a bounded portion and must still make a movement with it.

The sleeping three

Peter, James and John had seen Christ transfigured; now they are asked to remain and keep watch. They are physically near but unavailable. Luke says that they sleep from grief. Their failure is real, but grief prevents it from being reduced to indifference.

The garden contains more than one failure of help. Presence can fail by occupying another’s act; it can also fail through absence within proximity. The disciples’ grief makes them unavailable; the angel’s presence makes Christ more available to his own prayer. The angel is present without becoming central. They are present without being able to remain with him.

The angel strengthens him

Luke’s sequence is exact. The angel strengthens Christ; Christ remains in agony; he prays more earnestly; his sweat becomes like drops of blood falling to the ground. The answer does not lessen the agony. It enlarges Christ’s capacity to remain within it. Strengthening funds a more intense prayer, not an escape from bodily extremity. The will is not less human because it is strengthened; it becomes capable of its human act.

Whatever judgement is made about the passage’s earliest textual form, the angel and the bloody sweat share one textual fate and have been received together. The one who is helped is not made less exposed. Help adds capacity at the point where capacity has failed.

Mark the Monk writes that baptismal grace “becomes active” as the commandments are kept and “never ceases secretly to help us”. The action does not complete a deficient grace, but neither does grace make action superfluous. The angel neither supplies a better decision nor performs the prayer. He strengthens the human being whose act must remain his own.8

This creaturely assistance also opens towards the Johannine promise of “another Advocate”. The angel is not the Holy Spirit, but his manner of help prefigures the help of the Paraclete: one who abides alongside, teaches, recalls and enables without becoming a substitute for the human witness. The difference of identity remains; the resemblance lies in the form of assistance.9

Dionysius called Christ’s activity a “new theandric energy”. Maximus receives the phrase while refusing any confusion of the divine and human operations: one Christ acts through both natures, each remaining what it is. Gethsemane gives this grammar bodily form. Divine strength and human action cannot be divided into percentages. The strength is given; the prayer, the sweat and the rising remain human.10

What God wills and what violence inflicts

The cup comes to Christ under two irreducible descriptions. As violence, it is filled by Judas, the authorities and the executioners. As the concrete vocation to remain Son within what they do, it is received from the Father. The Father does not give betrayal, torture or murder. He gives the Son the vocation to remain in filial love within the violence that human freedom is permitted to inflict.

Maximus distinguishes what God wills by good pleasure from what enters providence by economy or concession. The distinction is not a device for calling evil secretly good. It protects the difference between God’s saving purpose and the acts that purpose takes up without authoring. The cup is not the catalogue of injuries but the form of filial fidelity within them. Violence attempts to extract Christ’s life; the Son gives what his enemies can only try to take.11

The distinction must remain absolute wherever Gethsemane is brought near human suffering. No one may appoint another person’s suffering as their cup. An assailant does not become the Father by forcing suffering upon someone. Where harm is imposed, restraint is not accompaniment but complicity, and removal is the duty. Nothing here underwrites an appeal to another person’s freedom as a reason not to intervene against abuse. Help must not appropriate the act that belongs to the person being helped; protection must interrupt the act that belongs to the person harming them.

He rises

Luke says that Christ rises from prayer. The verb, anastas, belongs to ordinary rising and also to Luke’s vocabulary of resurrection; the resonance should not be made into a prediction, but it is difficult not to hear Easter faintly within the movement. Strength passes into balance, muscle, orientation and step. The cup remains bitter, and the body stands.12

Grace becomes visible as gait.

The cup of his suffering becomes the cup of our salvation neither because suffering saves by its quantity nor because endurance makes violence holy. What is inflicted upon him enters an act that the violence itself cannot produce. Christ receives from the Father the form of fidelity; he bears human malice without allowing it to determine the meaning of his act; he gives his life where others intend only to take it.

The angel does not take the cup. Christ rises and receives it himself. The ground bore his body and the cup gave his suffering a form. Strength was added without the demand being altered, and the movement remained his.
