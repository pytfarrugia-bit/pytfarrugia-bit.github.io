Borrowed Bodies

Six stances of contact

Ancient statues gather movement into a still point: the pressure of a foot remains inside the stride; an arm extends and continues to desire what lies beyond it; another body has been lifted, carried, drawn close; a fighter sits after the contest, while the contest still turns within his flesh. The stone arrests the passage, yet the living body that enters its form soon discovers paths leading backwards and forwards from it, towards the movement that prepared the stance and towards the movement by which it might be completed.

This project places six ancient sculptures beside Ruella Frank’s fundamental movements of yielding-with, pushing-against, reaching-for, grasping-onto, pulling-toward and releasing-from. These movements turn through one another, each becoming the ground from which another may arise: yielding allows pressure to be received; pressure gathers the body sufficiently for a reach; the reach finds or fails to find its object; the hand closes, the other is drawn inward, and in time the hold changes so that separation becomes possible. The sequence may run cleanly, falter midway, or circle around an unfinished passage for years.

The clinical figure is the whole situation assembled around the sculpture: the ancient form and the person who approaches it; the chair, floor, object or weight; the distance and gaze of the therapist; the history carried in the joints; the effort by which the pose is entered; the feeling that arrives only after one small feature has been altered. Meaning emerges within this field, where the statue offers a temporary bodily organisation and the person discovers how their own body receives, revises or refuses it.

James Kepner gives the work its somatic discipline. Bodily process possesses its own actuality; the therapist stays near pressure, breath, muscular effort, balance, gesture and movement, allowing meaning to thicken from experience instead of fastening an interpretation upon the pose. Ruella Frank gives the six their developmental and relational course; each movement occurs with, against, for, onto, towards or from a world that answers it, and through whose answer the person gains some further sense of their own form.

Contents The Throne; The Step; The Offering; The Carry; The Nearness; The After-Fight

I. The Throne

Ruella Frank: yielding-with

Hatshepsut sits within an architecture of support. The throne receives the pelvis and thighs; the feet descend towards the ground; the spine rises through the middle of a body whose hands lie upon the legs. Her stillness contains weight, yet the weight has somewhere to travel, and the upright form seems to grow from that permission to descend. The throne becomes part of the body’s organisation, almost a second skeleton placed outside the skin, receiving what the muscles might otherwise attempt to carry alone.

To enter the form, the person sits far enough back for the chair to meet the pelvis and, where possible, the spine; the feet are placed fully upon the floor, while the hands rest upon the thighs and the gaze settles somewhere ahead. Attention begins where body and support touch: beneath the sitting bones, along the back of the legs, at the soles, perhaps behind the ribs or shoulder blades. Kepner’s phrase, “one’s legs as support”, becomes concrete here, since support is experienced as an answering pressure rather than an idea about stability.

A person may discover that the chair has remained largely unused; the thighs grip, the abdomen suspends the torso, the shoulders lift, and the back hovers a little forward as though the support might vanish if trusted. Another body may descend heavily into the chair and lose the thread that runs from pelvis through sternum, throat and crown. Between these arrangements lies the subtler labour of yielding-with: weight passes into the environment, while the environment returns enough support for alertness, differentiation and form.

The therapist belongs to this architecture. A frontal gaze may allow composure to gather, or it may turn the throne into a place of examination; increased distance may make yielding possible, while nearness may fill the chest or throat with effort. One alteration at a time can uncover the shape of the field: the person moves away from the backrest and then returns; the palms turn upwards or downwards; the eyes lower, close or meet another’s gaze; a folded cloth is placed behind the head or beneath the feet. Each change reveals how support, exposure, rank, permission and watchfulness have become plaited together.

When the person rises, the crucial instant occurs as the throne relinquishes the body and the legs assume its weight. The after-image may be one of height, relief, shame, emptiness or resistance; the body may miss the chair as soon as it leaves it, or feel newly able to stand because it has first allowed itself to be seated. The stance asks how support may be received while the person remains present within it; it also asks which forms of dignity become possible when uprightness grows from being borne.

II. The Step

Ruella Frank: pushing-against

The kouros stands at the beginning of a road. One foot has advanced, yet the torso remains gathered over the ground; the step opens distance without casting the body headlong into it. The visible movement belongs to the forward leg, though its force begins elsewhere, in the pressure of the rear foot, the answering firmness of the earth, and the line of organisation that travels upwards through ankle, knee, hip and spine.

The experiment begins with both feet beneath the body and with enough time for the soles to become distinct. Pressure is added gradually, first through both feet and then through one; the ground answers, and the person notices whether that answer reaches the pelvis and back or disappears into locked knees, lifted toes, clenched buttocks or a held breath. Frank’s instruction that support be built “from the bottom up” carries beyond the feeling for which she first gave it, since propulsion, boundary, speech and the right to advance all require some path by which force can rise from the ground.

Once the answering pressure becomes available, one foot moves forward and rests lightly, while the rear foot retains the memory of the place from which the movement began. The division of weight may feel poised and spacious; it may also expose a familiar dilemma in which departure means betrayal, advance means danger, or the rear foot clings to what the upper body insists upon leaving. Some bodies pull themselves forward from the chest and throat; others extend the foot ceremonially while withholding the transfer of weight that would turn gesture into travel.

The length of the step can be altered, as can the direction of the gaze, the position of the arms and the degree of pressure through the rear heel. A smaller step may carry more agency than a dramatic stride; a larger one may reveal how quickly intention becomes overextension. The person can allow the pelvis to cross the threshold between the feet, then return, discovering how movement changes when it is reversible. The therapist may stand ahead, to one side or behind; each position changes the road, since movement always takes place towards, away from or alongside some portion of the field.

Pushing-against forms differentiation through counterforce. The ground resists without withdrawing; because it remains, the body can discover its own strength against it. The step therefore concerns movement and relation together: the person advances while retaining contact with the place that made advance possible. When the feet return side by side, the body may feel gathered, thwarted or completed; the question is whether one can go forward through support, carrying the ground within the movement rather than leaving it behind.

III. The Offering

Ruella Frank: reaching-for

The kore extends her arms into the space before her, carrying an object across the distance between one body and another. The object gives the reach weight and direction; the gesture becomes a passage through which gift, request, worship, appeasement, invitation or obligation may travel. Her body remains behind the hands without retreating from them, and the form gathers the question of how desire becomes visible when it takes something with it.

Frank writes that “Reaching is a whole body event”; the sentence belongs closely to this figure, since the reach begins long before the fingers cross into the space beyond the torso. It begins in the feet and spine, in the widening or narrowing of the chest, in the orientation of eyes and ears, and in the body’s estimate of distance: how far away is what is wanted, and what degree of exposure will be required to approach it?

The person begins with a small object held near the sternum or abdomen. Having found some support in the feet and back, the arms extend by degrees; the shoulder blades move around the ribs, the elbows unfold, and the object enters the space between person and recipient. The therapist attends to the route of the movement. The hands may travel while the torso pulls backwards; the chin may reach first; the breath may stop as the object leaves the body’s protected centre. At other times the whole person pours forward, surrendering balance to ensure that the offering arrives.

The recipient’s response changes the gesture from within. A hand reaching to receive may ease the shoulders or increase urgency; a delayed response may cause the fingers to tighten around the object; a step backwards may turn the offering into pursuit, while sudden nearness may make the person withdraw before the object has been taken. The object itself can be removed while the arms remain extended, revealing what had travelled beneath the offering; it may have served as bridge, credential, payment, shield or proof that the person deserved to approach.

The experiment deepens when the person gives voice to the reach without fixing its meaning beforehand. A phrase such as “this is for you”, “will you take this?”, “see what I have brought” or “I need you to receive this” may alter the chest, jaw and hands more profoundly than a change of posture. The body then discovers whether its movement permits an answer, including refusal, or whether the entire gesture has become organised around securing one outcome.

As the arms return, the person notices whether the movement feels like completion, retraction or failure. A reach may end because the object has been received; it may also end because the person has recognised the limit of the other’s availability. The kore’s stance asks how desire may cross the interval between bodies while retaining its roots in the person who desires; it asks how something may be offered without fastening the recipient to the offering.

IV. The Carry

Ruella Frank: grasping-onto

The Moschophoros is formed around a living weight. The calf lies across the bearer’s shoulders; the animal’s limbs and the man’s arms cross before the chest, making a knot whose lines belong to two bodies at once. The hands close around what must be secured, while the back, ribs, pelvis and feet are reorganised by a burden that cannot be held by the fingers alone. Bearer and carried creature remain distinct, yet each enters the visible form of the other.

Frank describes the grasping infant who “takes firm hold of the other for leverage and support”; the words disclose something fundamental about holding, since the object of the grasp also helps organise the one who grasps. Through pressure, weight and resistance, the hand discovers both the other and itself; what is held becomes a source of information about strength, limit, distance and need.

A folded blanket, bolster or manageable weight can be placed across the shoulders or gathered against the upper body. The person finds a grip sufficient to keep it secure, then follows the distribution of effort through hands, forearms, shoulders, back and feet. The body may initially assign the whole task to the fingers and neck; with time, the elbows may descend, the ribs may broaden, and the ground may begin to carry what the hands had tried to manage alone.

The distinction between holding and gripping appears through small changes. The fingers loosen by a fraction; the object moves nearer the chest or a little farther away; one arm takes more weight before it is redistributed. A grip shaped by fear often continues beyond the demand of the object, as though safety depended upon perpetual contraction. A grip shaped by sufficient support can remain responsive, tightening as the weight shifts and softening when the burden settles. The body learns that security may reside in adjustment rather than in an unchanging clasp.

The carried object also alters the bearer’s sense of purpose. Weight may gather the body and give it coherence; it may produce resentment, pride, fatigue, tenderness or the fear that putting something down will expose it to harm. The therapist can ask how long the person imagines they have been carrying in this way, though the enquiry remains tethered to the present body: what is happening in the hands now, where does the breath go, what part of the floor has disappeared, and which muscle believes that release would be dangerous?

Lowering the object becomes part of the stance rather than an afterthought. The weight is transferred to a chair or floor while the hands remain in contact; only then do they open. The body may continue to carry the absent burden, shoulders lifted and arms curved around an emptiness whose shape remains exact. The Moschophoros asks how taking hold changes the holder; it asks whether care can include the ground, the back and the possibility of setting down, so that the hands need not become the sole guardians of what matters.

V. The Nearness

Ruella Frank: pulling-toward

Isis gathers the child towards her lap and breast; the reached-for body has entered the intimate circle of the torso, where touch, nourishment, warmth and rhythm pass between one body and another. The sculptural group holds two forms together without obscuring their distinction: one body supports and offers; the other receives, presses, turns and takes. Nearness appears as an achieved relation whose continuation depends upon the minute adjustments of both.

Kepner writes that “Embodiment is intrinsically bound to mutual, body-to-body social interaction”; the phrase gives this figure its clinical breadth, since the bodily memory of being held, fed, steadied, welcomed or intruded upon enters later forms of intimacy as a living ground.

The experiment begins with a cushion or weighted object held at some distance. The person establishes contact through the hands and gradually bends the elbows, drawing the object towards the abdomen or chest. Attention follows the shrinking interval; somewhere along that short journey, distance becomes nearness, and the body may soften, brace, hurry, numb itself or pause. The exact point matters more than the achievement of full contact, since a few centimetres may divide welcome from intrusion.

Once the object rests against the torso, the person notices how the rest of the body responds. The back may retain the chair or become rounded around what is held; the feet may remain clear or vanish from awareness; the arms may soften enough for the weight to be shared by the lap and chest, or may continue to hold as though the object had never arrived. Nearness becomes sustainable when the person’s own centre remains available within the embrace, allowing contact to deepen without requiring either body to disappear.

The clinical field extends from early feeding into friendship, sexuality, grief, prayer, care and every relation in which something valued is permitted to come close. Pulling-toward may gather nourishment; it may also gather what the person fears losing, turning intimacy into vigilance. The object can therefore be moved slightly away and brought back, while the person notices whether connection survives the reappearance of space. The therapist may ask whether closeness has a rhythm, whether it can breathe, and whether the held other possesses a direction of its own.

The movement outwards deserves the same care as the movement inward. The arms extend gradually, while the palms continue to sense the object and the gaze remains available; distance returns as part of the relationship, carrying the knowledge of contact rather than erasing it. Isis and Horus offer a form in which nourishment and distinction dwell together; the stance asks how another may be welcomed towards the body’s centre while each remains capable of weight, rhythm and departure.

VI. The After-Fight

Ruella Frank: releasing-from

The Boxer sits after exertion, wounded and heavy, with the contest still echoing through his organisation. His weight has reached the seat, and his forearms have descended towards his thighs; yet the turned head, the gathered shoulders and the density of the hands suggest a body whose attention remains partly elsewhere. The fight has ceased in the world around him; within the body, cessation has yet to find its full course.

This figure stands at the threshold of releasing-from, where the hold begins to alter but has not yet become rest. The person takes the seated form, feet apart and forearms supported, while the head turns towards an imagined sound or presence. Attention moves through the body, finding what has already stopped and what continues to work: the jaw may remain set, the hands heavy or closed, the abdomen prepared, the gaze fixed upon the edge from which the next demand might come.

Kepner’s statement that “Awareness alone does not produce change” becomes especially important here. Release may require repeated bodily action through which the nervous system encounters support, orientation and completion often enough for a different organisation to become available. The person therefore receives no command to relax; instead, one hand opens while the other remains closed, the feet press briefly into the floor and then lessen their pressure, the head returns by degrees towards the room, and the back discovers the chair behind it.

Each movement is small enough for the body to retain choice. Opening both hands at once may expose too much; sitting upright may remove a protective curve before the person has found another support; closing the eyes may deepen rest or abolish orientation. The therapist attends to these differences, allowing release to take the form of a path with turns and returns rather than a gate through which the body must pass in a single motion.

The form also permits the person to discover what unfinished action remains within the apparent rest. A foot may wish to push away; the shoulders may want to complete a blow, ward something off or curl around an injured centre; the head may be listening for recognition, instruction or danger. Giving the movement a small, contained completion may allow the seated body to change more deeply than an effort to become calm. The release then arises through fulfilment, support and discrimination, rather than through exhaustion alone.

Standing from the pose completes the experiment. The person looks around the room, receives its boundaries, and notices whether the legs rise towards another encounter, away from the former one, or simply into the present. The Boxer asks what the body requires before an event can pass from immediate preparation into memory; he asks how rest becomes possible when the fight has ended outside the body before it has ended within it.

Method of use

Each stance begins with looking, since the sculpture should first be encountered as an arrangement of weight, direction, support and relation; the person then enters the form slowly, receives what the environment gives back, remains long enough for pressure and feeling to become distinct, alters one feature, and leaves with equal deliberation. The transition into the pose may reveal where agency falters; the transition out may disclose what the body has feared losing or what it has waited to complete.

These six bodies form a turning path rather than a ladder: the throne yields into the ground; the kouros presses and advances; the kore extends something across the interval; the bearer takes hold and is shaped by what he carries; Isis draws the other towards the centre; the Boxer searches for the movement by which contact, exertion and danger may become past. The living body enters each chamber for a time, discovering within an inherited form some movement of its own that has been waiting, perhaps quietly and for many years, to be felt through.

Texts

Ruella Frank, “Reaching and Being Reached”; James Kepner, “Towards a More Deeply Embodied Approach in Gestalt Therapy”.
