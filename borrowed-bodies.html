Borrowed Bodies

Six stances of contact

Ancient statues gather movement into a still point: pressure remains inside a stride, an arm continues to desire what lies beyond it, a carried body reorganises the one who bears it, and a fighter sits while the contest still turns within his flesh. The living body that enters such a form finds paths leading backwards towards the movement that prepared it and forwards towards what might complete it.

To borrow a body is to take on, for a time, the organisation of a body not one’s own. The form is neither copied as theatre nor claimed as identity; it is received, tested and handed back, leaving the borrower more aware of what their own body accepted, altered or refused.

This project places six sculptures beside Ruella Frank’s movements of yielding-with, pushing-against, reaching-for, grasping-onto, pulling-toward and releasing-from. James Kepner supplies the somatic discipline: stay near pressure, support, effort and movement, allowing meaning to thicken from the body rather than fastening an interpretation onto the pose.

Method of use

Begin by looking. Enter the form slowly and remain until pressure, effort and feeling become distinct. Alter one feature at a time. The therapist’s gaze, position and distance belong to the field as surely as the chair, floor or object. Entry and exit are both data: the movement into a pose reveals where agency falters, while leaving it discloses what the body feared losing or waited to complete. Meaning emerges from these changes and is not imposed upon them.

The six movements form a turning path rather than a ladder. Yielding makes pressure receivable; pressure gathers the body for a reach; the reach meets or misses its object; the hand closes, the other is drawn inward, and the hold changes so that separation becomes possible. The sequence also stalls, doubles back or circles around an unfinished passage.

I. The Throne

Ruella Frank: yielding-with

Support, exposure, rank and permission have become plaited together. A queen sits within an architecture of support: the throne receives her pelvis and thighs, her feet descend, and the spine rises because weight has somewhere to travel. The throne acts almost as a second skeleton outside the skin, carrying what the muscles would otherwise hold alone.

The chair easily remains unused. The thighs grip, the torso hovers and the back refuses contact, as though support might vanish if trusted. At the opposite extreme, the body descends so fully that the thread of uprightness disappears. Yielding-with lies between collapse and self-suspension: weight passes into the environment, which returns enough pressure for alertness and form.

Protocol

Sit far enough back for the chair to meet the pelvis and, where possible, the spine. Place both feet fully on the floor and rest the hands on the thighs. Notice the answering pressure. Move away from the backrest and return; turn the palms; lower or meet the gaze; place a folded cloth behind the head or beneath the feet. Change one feature at a time.

Rising reveals what the seat had carried. The legs assume the weight, and the after-image arrives as height, relief, shame or resistance. The body misses the chair as soon as it leaves it.

II. The Step

Ruella Frank: pushing-against

Departure means betrayal, advance means danger. A youth stands at the threshold of the road, one foot advanced while the torso remains gathered over the ground. The visible movement belongs to the forward leg, but its force begins in the rear foot and the earth’s answering resistance.

Pushing-against forms differentiation through counterforce. Some bodies pull themselves forward from the chest; others extend the foot ceremonially while withholding the transfer of weight that would turn gesture into travel. A smaller step often carries more agency than a dramatic stride because it remains reversible.

Protocol

Begin with both feet beneath the body. Add pressure through both soles and then through one. Move one foot forward while the rear foot keeps contact with the ground. Alter the length of the step, the gaze, the arms and pressure through the rear heel. Let the pelvis cross the threshold between the feet and return. Change the therapist’s position from ahead to beside or behind.

When the feet meet again, the body carries the ground within the movement rather than leaving it behind.

III. The Offering

Ruella Frank: reaching-for

The object may have served as bridge, credential, payment, shield or proof that the person deserved to approach. A votary extends an offering into the interval between bodies. The object gives the reach weight and direction; gift, request and obligation travel through the same gesture.

The recipient changes the reach from within. A hand moving forward eases the shoulders or increases urgency; delay tightens the fingers; a step backwards turns the offering into pursuit. Remove the object while the arms remain extended and the movement beneath it becomes visible. The reach then asks for an answer without securing one.

Protocol

Hold a small object near the sternum or abdomen. Find support in the feet and back, then extend the arms by degrees while the therapist follows the route of the movement. Pause if the torso withdraws or pours forward. Remove the object while the arms remain extended. Speak one simple phrase of offering or request, then allow the arms to return without deciding beforehand whether the movement means completion or refusal.

Returning the arms distinguishes completion from retraction. Desire crosses the interval while retaining its roots in the body that desires; the offering reaches another without fastening them to it.

IV. The Carry

Ruella Frank: grasping-onto

Safety depended upon perpetual contraction. A herdsman is formed around a living weight: the calf lies across his shoulders, and the crossed limbs of animal and bearer make a knot belonging to two bodies at once. The burden reorganises the back and feet; the hands cannot hold it alone.

Frank describes the grasping infant who “takes firm hold of the other for leverage and support”. What is held also organises the holder. Holding responds to shifts in weight; gripping continues beyond the demand of the object, as though any softening would expose it to harm. The distinction appears in fractions of movement.

Protocol

Place a folded blanket, bolster or manageable weight across the shoulders or against the upper body. Find a grip sufficient to secure it and follow how the task distributes through the body. Loosen the fingers slightly, move the weight nearer and farther, and let one arm take more before redistributing it. Lower the object to a chair or floor while the hands remain in contact; only then open them.

After the object is lowered, the shoulders stay lifted and the arms curve around an emptiness whose shape remains exact.

V. The Nearness

Ruella Frank: pulling-toward

A few centimetres may divide welcome from intrusion. A goddess gathers the child towards her lap and breast, where nourishment and rhythm pass between two bodies without dissolving their distinction. Nearness is not a fixed distance but an achieved relation sustained through minute adjustment.

As an object approaches the torso, the back either retains support or rounds around what is held. The arms soften enough for the lap and chest to share the weight, or continue to labour as though contact had never arrived. Pulling-toward gathers nourishment; it also gathers what one fears losing, turning intimacy into vigilance. Friendship and prayer both depend upon whether closeness permits space to return.

Protocol

Begin with a cushion or weighted object at some distance. Establish contact through the hands, then bend the elbows and draw it towards the abdomen or chest. Pause where distance changes into nearness. Let the object rest against the torso and notice whether the back, feet and arms remain available. Move it slightly away and return it. Then extend the arms slowly while the palms continue to sense its surface.

Distance returns as part of the relation rather than its failure, the palms still sensing as the interval opens.

VI. The After-Fight

Ruella Frank: releasing-from

The Boxer does not depict release. A beaten fighter sits after exertion, wounded and heavy, while the turned head and gathered shoulders keep part of him inside the contest. The fight has ended around him; cessation has not yet completed itself within him.

Kepner writes that “Awareness alone does not produce change”. Release requires bodily action through which support, orientation and completion become available often enough for another organisation to emerge. The body receives no command to relax. It discovers what has stopped and what continues to prepare.

Protocol

Sit with the feet apart and forearms supported on the thighs. Turn the head towards an imagined sound. Open one hand while the other remains closed; press the feet briefly and then lessen the pressure; return the head by degrees and let the back find the chair. Stand, look around the room and receive its boundaries. Keep each movement small enough for choice to remain present.

An unfinished action often lives inside apparent rest: a foot still pushes, a shoulder wards off, the head listens for danger. A small completion changes the seated body more deeply than an effort to become calm. What does the body require before an event can pass from immediate preparation into memory?

Texts

Ruella Frank, “Reaching and Being Reached”; James Kepner, “Towards a More Deeply Embodied Approach in Gestalt Therapy”.

These bodies are not ethically level. Borrowing a pharaoh’s enthronement is not the same act as borrowing a votary’s approach, a goddess’s nursing or a beaten fighter’s exhaustion. Each form is entered provisionally, with attention to the history and asymmetry carried by the body one borrows, and then handed back.
