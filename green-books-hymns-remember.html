What the Hymns Remember

On mothers, praise, and forms of care

A reading of Francis X. Clooney’s Divine Mother, Blessed Mother: Hindu Goddesses and the Virgin Mary (2005).

Francis Clooney warns that hymns yield little to a reader who wants information from them. A hymn speaks to someone. Whoever takes it seriously approaches the relation it enacts, and risks being drawn in. His book is built accordingly. Hindu hymns of praise stand beside Marian ones, goddess and Mother of God read in pairs, and the reader is asked to cross over and come home changed by what the unfamiliar text has made newly audible. Somewhere in the middle chapters it occurred to me that this is what these essays have been attempting by instinct. Clooney does it deliberately, with a scholar’s patience, accepting that the reader who crosses over cannot fully control what returns with him.

I followed him by listening, in the evenings, with the Little Office open on the table beside me. I came with a deep respect for dhrupad and the rudra veena, but these recordings belonged to other devotional idioms. What I heard was neither wholly foreign nor familiar. It arrived like news from a country adjacent to one I had lived in. The hymns felt less like new doctrine than like rememberings, returning things I had once known how to want.

The first was the Śrī Guṇaratna Kośa, Parāśara Bhaṭṭar’s Sanskrit praise of Śrī at Srirangam. Sung by a man, and playful. Śrī teases and persuades. Her mercy is clever rather than ponderous, and the divine couple appear in majesty and also in pleasure, in wit, in abundance. Then I saw Ranganāyaki at Srirangam, seated beneath flowers, textiles, and jewellery until the figure seemed almost made of offerings. It looked like what hyperdulia might produce if it were allowed to become material.

The theological difference is absolute. Śrī is divine. Mary is a creature. Yet the image exposed how narrow my own expectations had grown. I have known Mary as refuge, obedience, and sorrow. These are true, but in my formation they had thickened into a complete weather system in which holiness is always grave and even splendour arrives dressed for a funeral.

This narrowness is not Marian praise as such. Clooney shows the point by setting Bhaṭṭar beside the Akathistos. The Greek hymn greets Mary with an extravagance that shades into wit: the unwedded bride, the container of the uncontainable, the space of the spaceless God. The praise piles up past every need, delighting in its own excess. The foreign hymn did not supply what my tradition lacked. It sent me home to a room of my own house I had stopped entering.

This should have been less surprising. The Father of her Son is the source of excess. Creation is unnecessary abundance, and at Cana there is more wine than the wedding can drink. I cannot turn this into a doctrine of Marian delight, but the intuition now has weight: it would be strange if the Mother nearest this prodigality were untouched by it. I noticed what the thought did in my chest. I have longed for care and distrusted many of the forms in which it arrived. Play felt frivolous for years. Being forgiven I could believe. Being enjoyed felt almost indecent. Śrī’s hymn, and behind it the Akathist, remembered another possibility: care may delight that someone exists.

The Saundarya Laharī began differently. Its opening claim is startling: Śiva can act only when united with Śakti. The feminine is honoured as power itself, the moving principle without which nothing moves. Clooney’s comparison makes the boundary more important, not less. Devī is not Mary, and God needs no complementary principle.

Christian theology makes another claim. Aquinas argues that the Annunciation precedes the Incarnation so that the Virgin’s consent might be sought and given on behalf of the whole human race. Her yes is personal without being private. Daughter Zion’s long covenantal answer becomes the free and bodily act of one woman. The Word receives his humanity from her. She conceives, gestates, and gives birth.

Mary is perfectly receptive because her receptivity is her act. Grace does not use her and set her aside. It brings her creaturely agency to its highest intensity, to the point at which the Word receives a body from her. This image is easily corrupted into a demand that mothers, and women generally, animate everyone around them. What the hymn remembered was smaller and better. Receptivity can be intelligent. Consent can change the world. Care may help life take form without being consumed by the forming.

With the Sulamangalam Sisters singing the Apirāmi Antāti, the atmosphere softened. Two women carried the hymn with a tenderness steadier than play, and I wondered whether delight had turned into labour. The form answered me. In an antāti, the last word of each verse becomes the first word of the next. One ending is another beginning. The praise circles, resumes, and finds another way to address her. The work belongs to the singers, but Apirāmi does not dissolve into their singing. She remains the one addressed, the one who can be addressed again because the relation had not been broken in the interval. The play has accepted repetition and become dependable.

Clooney’s pairing reached furthest into my own practice here, because the Marian hymn beside Apirāmi’s is itself an antāti, the Mātāracammaṉ Antāti, Tamil Catholic praise of Mary in the same linked-verse form. The circling return I thought I was borrowing already existed within Marian devotion, in a language and poetics I had never met. Once seen there, it appeared everywhere at home. The rosary repeats. The Angelus repeats. The Little Office waits through the seasons with its appointed antiphons. On difficult days this feels exacting, while on better days it is a relief that nothing need be invented. The canticles stay where they were left. The prayer can be neglected and resumed.

For a long time I believed every interruption destroyed the bond, and that return required apology, proof, or the recovery of an emotional state gone cold. Hearing the sisters hand each verse its next beginning, I recognised that repeated prayer had been contradicting this in me for years. The work of attention belongs to the one who prays. The relationship is held from the other side. Care may survive one’s departure, and the proof is that the antiphon is still there in the morning.

Clooney pairs the Saundarya Laharī with the Stabat Mater. I heard the latter through Vivaldi, and let it come last. It carried a different weight. There was none of Śrī’s play, Devī’s movement, or the antāti’s circling tenderness. Mary stands beneath what cannot be prevented or repaired, and Vivaldi refuses to let the sorrow hurry. The phrases lower themselves into the same place and remain there. This stillness is itself a work, and a severe one. To stay beside suffering without converting it into explanation or consolation is among the hardest forms of presence I know, in the consulting room as much as at the foot of the Cross. Care may be unable to lift the weight. It can still refuse to leave the person beneath it alone.

Clooney offers no composite Mother, and neither do these hearings. Śrī, Devī, Apirāmi, and Mary remain themselves. Their differences made audible four wishes older than definition: to be delighted in, helped into life, permitted to return, and not abandoned beneath an unlifted weight. No human mother could be required to bear all four without being crushed. They are not definitions of what a mother ought to be. They are wishes the body formed before it could explain them, and the hymns, mine and not mine alike, know their names.

The book has gone back to the shelf. The Little Office is open on the table where it was before I started, and tomorrow’s antiphon is already appointed. I will begin again there.
